Interviewer: I sent you the questionnaire earlier. I’ve also got a scanned copy here. Shall we go through it together?
Interviewee: Yes, let’s go through it.
Interviewer: Perfect. Thanks again for your time – we’ll try to keep it short. The project is funded by the Slovenian Research Agency, lasts three years, and the aim is to understand the state of the transition towards a circular economy in Slovenia. There’s a lot of talk about it, but we want to see what is really happening.
Interviewee: There are many small, individual projects. Of course, the EU sets directives that Slovenia should follow. But on a higher level – the mentality of Slovenians isn’t yet there. Technically and engineering-wise we know the topics – waste, electricity, water – but the mindset for full symbiosis is still missing.
Interviewer: Great. Could you tell me about your location – where are you based, and what are the advantages?
Interviewee: We’re almost in the center of Ljubljana, which is logistically excellent. We can reach anyone quickly. From a European perspective, Slovenia is well positioned as a hub for Southeast Europe. Lately, we’ve also collaborated a lot with Austrian partners. Slovenia has relatively low labor costs and high service quality, which gives us great price-performance.
Interviewer: That’s definitely a competitive edge, especially for foreign markets. How do you see the legislation on waste management, recycling, secondary materials? Does it help or hinder?
Interviewee: Slovenian law follows EU directives, but it isn’t proactive. Policymakers lack ambition and broad vision. For example, I studied the PPWR packaging regulation closely during my doctorate – lobbying really watered it down. I think legislation should be clearer and stricter if we want real progress, not just PR stories.
Interviewer: Looking at project implementation – what are the key factors? Economic or environmental?
Interviewee: Economics comes first. If a project doesn’t bring financial benefit, companies won’t commit. Legislation comes second – enforcement helps. Then there’s marketing – the “green story” sells well. Environmental motives come fourth. That’s the reality.
Interviewer: How do you evaluate efforts to reduce emissions, water use, waste – nationally and locally?
Interviewee: Some measures exist – e.g., subsidies from Eco Fund. But public transport in Ljubljana should be free – then it would be widely used. Remote work should be legally encouraged – one day a week would significantly cut emissions. What’s missing is political courage and determination.
Interviewer: How strong are business connections in Slovenia around sustainability?
Interviewee: Fairly closed circles. You see the same faces at sustainability events. There’s little knowledge exchange. Civil society is poorly included. We need more awareness in schools, universities, and companies.
Interviewer: Do companies also exchange materials, like secondary raw materials?
Interviewee: Yes – with LUŠT, for example, we made packaging from tomato stems: bags, cartons, even boxes for the EU Presidency. This reduced their costs and created value from waste. We’ve got more than 20 such cases across Slovenia.
Interviewer: Does Slovenia have enough expertise for industrial symbiosis?
Interviewee: Unfortunately not. Until recently, you couldn’t even study sustainability technologies here. Most experts are self-taught or trained abroad. We lack programs, policies, and career paths for young professionals.
Interviewer: Who are your key partners? How do collaborations start?
Interviewee: Big foreign companies are the most serious – they have long-term strategies. Knauf is an example. Also start-ups – often because sustainability is required for funding calls. Collaborations often begin with projects and then develop further.
Interviewer: How do you build trust with new partners?
Interviewee: Very important. In our sector, everyone knows everyone. If you prove yourself in one project, word spreads fast. Trust usually starts formally but deepens over time. Without personal relations, long-term collaboration is hard.
Interviewer: What about formal vs. informal cooperation?
Interviewee: Mostly formal. Informal cooperation is rare. Large companies are cautious, worried about information leaks.
Interviewer: Within industrial symbiosis networks – is trust based on contracts or informality?
Interviewee: Depends. If the idea looks promising, we sometimes start without a contract, just based on mutual interest. But in regular cases, we set a price, lab costs, and move forward. For innovative ideas, we sometimes work “on goodwill,” because we believe in them.
Interviewer: How could industrial symbiosis networks be improved?
Interviewee: More promotion of good practices – there’s a lot going on, but it isn’t visible enough. The state should support sustainability more directly – e.g., tax breaks for circular economy investments. Infrastructure is also key – eco-parks, timber construction, stronger coordination.
Interviewer: Finally, do you see opportunities to improve efficiency in your own organization?
Interviewee: Definitely. We plan a larger photovoltaic unit. More remote work would also cut costs. Our building needs an energy renovation. But we already do quite a lot to stay as sustainable as possible.
