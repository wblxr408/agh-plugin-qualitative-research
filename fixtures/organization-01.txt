Organization: 1
Interviewer:
What is your organizational structure, can you describe it?
Interviewee:
Yes, our center is an umbrella organization under which there are several institutes that cover natural sciences, social sciences, and humanities. We also have some infrastructure units… I mean, those that provide support to these institutes. For example, our institute has a laboratory that deals with the analysis of biological raw materials, mainly from a chemical perspective. We also have a center that provides support for field research, manages experimental fields, and field equipment. The laboratory is also accredited, I think by the Slovenian Accreditation body, and it is a reference laboratory in the field of our raw materials.
Interviewer:
Would you be willing to be part of an industrial symbiosis network?
Interviewee:
Look, we are very active in projects. For example, in the processing of biological raw materials, by-products are created. For 20 years, we’ve been striving to reuse them. Before 2022, however, according to legislation, they were still considered waste. From an environmental point of view, this is quite demanding, because the criteria are very high, sometimes in my opinion unjustifiably high.
And, yes… this also personally bothers me. For example, when textbooks state that agriculture is the biggest polluter… well, then what are we going to eat? Children then think of it as something very negative.
We have a project where we use residues from the food industry – moving towards developing fertilizer. We also have a project where we try to prove that these substances, although legislation classifies them as waste after seven days, are not actually as problematic as is assumed.
Interviewer:
What are your current and future sustainability goals?
Interviewee:
Look, research can prove many things, but before it reaches industry… it takes time. On the declarative level, Slovenia is good, but when it comes to transfer into practice, funding runs out, and there is no interest.
One example: in Slovenian Istria – many organizations have biological raw materials. They could have one joint depot. One truck, one composting facility. Another company does have a composting facility, but it has no capacity. Others aren’t interested, because they already pay for waste removal.
It seems to me that there has not yet been an individual with a good enough idea to connect all this.
And for example, olive leaves – we even import them now, although we have enough at home.
Interviewer:
What is the support of the community, the advantages of the location?
Interviewee:
The community supports us. We cooperate with municipalities. But… often the final step is missing, for example concrete financial support.
In Italy, this is well arranged. Because there, the regions recognize their specificities. Here we have the same rules for everyone, but the climate differences are huge.
Interviewer:
How do you assess the legislative framework?
Interviewee:
The main problem – there is no continuity. People at the ministries change, and you keep explaining the same things over and over again. Processes drag on.
Another thing – Slovenia is small. We cannot compare ourselves with larger countries, but the legislation is the same. For example: our fishermen with small boats are not the same as Italians or Spaniards with large boats. The same in agriculture. And the system is too rigid, adjustments are difficult.
Interviewer:
Which factors influence the success of sustainability projects?
Interviewee:
Above all, motivation. You can have all the resources, but if there is no will, nothing will happen. The motivation of the individual is important, but also organizational support.
Interviewer:
What are Slovenia’s measures to reduce CO₂, water, waste?
Interviewee:
It is trying, yes. But bureaucracy stops a lot.
Example: one valley in Slovenia – they wanted to set up an irrigation system, but the land tenants, the fund, legislation… everything got complicated. Organization is a bigger problem than money. Although in the end – money rules the world.
Interviewer:
How do you assess sustainable relations and information exchange?
Interviewee:
It depends on individuals. There is no systemic regulation. If relations are good – we cooperate. If not – it doesn’t work.
Cooperation is based on trust from informal circles. If everything were formalized, there would be greater success.
Interviewer:
Is there an exchange of products, for example for other processes?
Interviewee:
Yes, we have an example with one company. We organized a Festival, without additional funds, with the same interest.
The raw material went into another process. We also added an exhibition and a concert. The Festival has now been running for several years.
Now I also hold an important position at an institute that was established last year – the initiative came from the local environment. They needed knowledge tailored to them, not universal solutions.
I also find interesting a company that sells even competitors’ products in its boutique – these are soft skills.
Connecting is key. Alone we can do nothing.
Interviewer:
What human capital is needed?
Interviewee:
It’s not about formal education. The ability to connect and to speak is important.
I was at a lecture by the Manager of the Year 2023 – she said that psychological tests are not enough. Soft skills are even more important. Even a researcher, if they lack soft skills, can be a problem.
Interviewer:
Who are your key partners?
Interviewee:
We come from the field. We have applied research. We build on existing partnerships, but we are also open to new ones.
Currently, we are quite busy, because there are too few of us. Projects come and go, it’s hard to get staff. Salaries are not high.
When we prepare a project ourselves, we choose partners according to the content.
We have some permanent, some new – about 50:50.
Interviewer:
How important is trust?
Interviewee:
Very important. We have a circle with whom we have been cooperating for 10, 20 years. They help us expand the network. Unfortunately, there are also those who do not see the potential.
Interviewer:
What is your opinion on formal/informal cooperation?
Interviewee:
We have an institute – we want to cooperate, but on their side there is no interest.
We are a public research institute, they are… established by the state. We want to merge – it would be more sustainable, more rational. But there is no interest.
Also among stakeholders – everyone “plays solo.” Too little coordination. We would need someone to connect this. If someone were paid for it, it would work.
Interviewer:
How do you assess trust among stakeholders?
Interviewee:
We did a survey – 74% would cooperate in a knowledge center.
Stakeholders want one point – to go there and get all the information. Now there is too much bureaucracy.
Interviewer:
Your suggestions for improving industrial symbiosis?
Interviewee:
Networking between legislation, industry, and research should be formalized.
Now the meaning of research is lost, because it cannot be transferred to industry due to legislation.
Example – water from treatment plants for irrigation. Legally prohibited. Even if the water is good, it’s not allowed.
Interviewer:
Can you further improve efficiency?
Interviewee:
Yes, for example by merging our segment.
Example from hop-growing – 100 hop-growers and an institute – they cooperate very well. For us, it is harder, because there are fewer stakeholders.
The question is how to organize and supervise all this.
