Organization: 2
Interviewer:
Hello, can you first describe the structure of your company?
Interviewee:
Of course. Our company operates in the field of aluminum processing – aluminum is our primary material. We are production-oriented and currently employ around 50 people. One of our key characteristics is that we manage the entire value chain – from development, production, and sales, all the way to assembly. There are no intermediaries in the whole process. We develop the product ourselves, manufacture it, and then deliver it directly to the end user. That is one of the specific features of our business model.
Interviewer:
Would you be willing to participate in an industrial symbiosis network that involves sharing financial inputs and results with other stakeholders in the environment?
Interviewee:
We are certainly open to such cooperation, but first, we would need to identify which resources would actually be useful for us – either as raw materials or in some other form. In the end, such cooperation also has to make financial sense. It’s true that we want to act sustainably, but for most companies this is still quite an unknown. There are no clear guidelines, principles, or processes… so most companies operate along established tracks.
In our case, we haven’t yet seen such systemic cooperation in the environment, but we would like to in the future. Today, companies mostly connect because of profit. Sustainability? That is still quite a marginal motivation. Cooperation is often selfish – everyone looks out for themselves, how to sell more, how to be more competitive.
Interviewer:
What forms of sustainable relations exist at the local level where you operate? Are you yourself involved in them? Which regulations do you follow when forming sustainable relations?
Interviewee:
Honestly speaking, from the point of view of recycling and legislation, we don’t deal with this much. Probably because it doesn’t have a major impact on our balance sheet. Our waste is minimal – aluminum is a sustainable material and we almost completely use it up. We don’t have surpluses such as heat, nor do we currently have a need for additional inputs from other companies.
At the moment, we are in the process of becoming energy independent. The first half of our solar power plant is already operating, and the second is being installed across the production hall. Our goal is to cover all of our energy needs ourselves. Is that sustainable? Of course. But it primarily comes from a business calculation – firstly, we are entering the market of substructures for solar power plants, which means we are also showing the market that we master this field. And secondly, we calculated that we have around €70,000 in annual electricity costs. With our own production, we will reduce these costs. Sustainability is thus the result of a rational business decision.
Interviewer:
What is your opinion on the current support of the local community and existing forms of sustainable cooperation?
Interviewee:
At the local level, for example from the municipality, we almost never talk about environmental issues. The municipality asks us whether we need additional land or infrastructure, but no one asks about emissions, waste, or water. In my opinion, this is wrong. The impulses should come from the outside – from the local community, the state, legislation. Without that, companies remain passive. Even when impulses do exist, companies often don’t even notice them.
Interviewer:
How do you assess existing legislation regarding recycling and reuse? What impact does it have on your business?
Interviewee:
In my opinion, it will take quite some time before sustainability policies are effectively implemented. Companies usually only start acting when deadlines are already near. Just remember the case of GDPR – everyone only started acting at the very last moment.
Interviewer:
Which factors do you see as key for the successful implementation of sustainable projects – environmental or economic factors?
Interviewee:
For me, the key factors are mainly economic. Not environmental. The goal of a company is to generate financial benefits. That is the basic logic of business.
Interviewer:
In your opinion, how does Slovenia or your region contribute to reducing emissions, water consumption, and the amount of waste?
Interviewee:
These topics are certainly important, but they are not in the foreground. For us, energy self-sufficiency is currently the most important sustainability activity – but even this is primarily motivated from the economic side.
Interviewer:
Are there currently any forms of information or knowledge exchange in the framework of sustainable relations?
Interviewee:
No, we do not currently have such exchanges. The impulses for sustainability measures come from the market – from customers. They demand sustainable living, and we respond by developing products such as substructures for solar panels, canopies, etc. But all of this is still viewed through the lens of profit.
Interviewer:
Are there any examples in your environment of exchanging inputs between companies for other production processes?
Interviewee:
No, we currently do not see such cooperation.
Interviewer:
What human capital is needed for successful industrial symbiosis? Does such human capital exist in your environment?
Interviewee:
I believe the initiative must come from the top – from the director, the management. These are long-term shifts. In my previous job, we were writing a sustainability strategy, but we didn’t really know what we were writing. Sustainability is a complex concept, it includes an internal and external dimension – and most companies don’t even understand this external one. Such topics should be part of regular meetings, but they are usually replaced by topics like sales, revenues, costs.
Interviewer:
Who are your key partners and how do you cooperate with them?
Interviewee:
We have established supply channels and work with suppliers. The key is to develop together with them. For example, when developing an outdoor kitchen, we needed stone slabs. Instead of going to a stonemason, we turned to the largest stone producer in the world, from Spain. Their engineers worked with us for half a year, came to Slovenia, and developed a product that fit our needs. However – this was not sustainable cooperation, it was development.
Interviewer:
How important is trust in establishing new collaborations?
Interviewee:
Trust is crucial – both from suppliers and customers. We have an example when a client from Austria visited us. At first, they didn’t trust us as a company from the Balkans. They wanted to see the production. Only after the visit and inspection did they sign the contract.
Interviewer:
What is your opinion on formal and informal cooperation?
Interviewee:
Everything that is initially informal must sooner or later be formalized – delivery conditions, contracts, everything has to be written down. The customer must sign, not just say: “Great, agreed.”
Interviewer:
How do you assess the level of trust among stakeholders in symbiotic networks?
Interviewee:
Trust must be very high. If someone gives you a raw material that they themselves see as waste, you must trust them that they handled it properly. But this also applies to ordinary suppliers – it’s a resource you have to integrate into your process.
Interviewer:
Do you have any suggestions for improving industrial symbiotic networks?
Interviewee:
I miss impulses – especially from the local community and legislation. Even just in the form of information, so you at least know that something is happening.
Interviewer:
Do you think your company can further improve efficiency and savings?
Interviewee:
Of course, there are reserves – in processes, in material utilization... But at the moment we are not actively focused on optimization. We are small, the process is simple. We would need an additional person dedicated to this. But that person has to bring in enough to make their work worthwhile.
And one more thing – companies today are too profit-oriented. That doesn’t necessarily mean it’s right. The whole system is based on the paradigm of growth, which might not be the best criterion of success.
