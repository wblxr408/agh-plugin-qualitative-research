Organization: 13
Interviewer:
I’ve started recording. Do you agree with the recording?
Interviewee:
Yes.
Interviewer:
Great, thanks. Let’s begin with our questionnaire. This research is about industrial symbiosis. Normally, companies exchange products through standard business relationships. In industrial symbiosis, however, the focus is on exchanging surplus resources: materials, energy, water, steam, etc. It can go beyond standard contracts into long-term cooperation.
Could you describe your organizational structure?
Interviewee:
We have 25 employees. The structure is split into two parts, also geographically:
A technical sector (production, materials, warehouse, delivery, etc.)
A sales and administrative sector (shop, accounting, marketing, support services).
Interviewer:
Would being part of an industrial symbiosis network interest you? For example, using surplus steam, heat, or materials from others—or offering your own surplus to others?
Interviewee:
Yes, if the infrastructure was already in place so we didn’t bear all the costs. Why waste surplus if someone else can use it?
Interviewer:
What would be the main motivator for joining? Environmental benefits, cost savings, efficiency?
Interviewee:
Primarily cost savings. We have surplus high-quality materials, but we can’t use them in our products. We pay to dispose of them even though they’re perfectly usable elsewhere. This costs us money, takes up storage space, and is a waste environmentally. The first driver would be reducing costs and clearing space. Environmental benefits come second.
Interviewer:
Has this issue always been present?
Interviewee:
No. It became significant after 2008 when we expanded production in-house (e.g., classic mattresses). With higher sales, surplus materials grew.
Interviewer:
Have you seen such collaborations elsewhere?
Interviewee:
Yes, some of our business partners abroad. It’s more common there, especially in industries where secondary materials can be used. Here in Slovenia, less so.
Interviewer:
Could smaller volumes be the barrier?
Interviewee:
Possibly. Larger buyers prefer sourcing from fewer suppliers with large volumes. Smaller companies like us may not be attractive, even if the material quality is high.
Interviewer:
How do you currently handle surpluses?
Interviewee:
We’re obliged to hand them to waste management companies (like Snaga). We have contracts. But essentially we pay them for disposal, and they may later resell the material profitably. So they charge us as intermediaries.
Interviewer:
Would a matchmaking service/platform help?
Interviewee:
Yes! Ideally a central service or platform where companies can list surplus materials and others can claim them. That would save us time and allow us to avoid paying waste companies. For example, we thought of collecting old textiles from customers (bed linen, 2–3kg bundles). Perfectly clean and reusable—for industrial rags, insulation, or recycling into fibers. But we couldn’t find a partner in Slovenia.
Interviewer:
So the main barrier is lack of information and time?
Interviewee:
Exactly. We don’t have staff dedicated to finding such partners. If there were an official platform or service, we’d use it immediately.
Interviewer:
Do regulations restrict you strongly?
Interviewee:
Not really. More inconvenience than restriction. Materials take up space, so we sort them to make collection easier and cheaper. Hazardous waste we don’t generate much of.
Interviewer:
Have you already practiced a form of symbiosis?
Interviewee:
Yes, with transparent plastic packaging film. We bale it and send it to a partner who uses it as an input material. We don’t pay for disposal, they get a raw material. A win-win. We’d love to expand this to other materials.
Interviewer:
Do local authorities or organizations provide support or information?
Interviewee:
Not really. There are some EU-funded “green” calls and circular economy programs, but for small firms they’re abstract and impractical. No concrete information or matchmaking help.
Interviewer:
So internal leadership and individuals matter a lot?
Interviewee:
Yes. It depends on the personal initiative of individuals. Our leadership is open-minded, so we try where possible. Without that, nothing would move.
Interviewer:
And your partners? Large or small firms?
Interviewee:
Foreign suppliers are usually larger, sometimes multinationals. Slovenian ones tend to be small or medium-sized (up to 50 employees). We find partners mostly through recommendations from trusted business contacts. Quality is the key. Environmental standards (certifications, etc.) are nice to have but not decisive.
Interviewer:
Have customers demanded more sustainability?
Interviewee:
Not really. Mostly only about packaging—do we need so much. Some partners promote climate neutrality or rainforest-friendly credentials, but it’s not a strong purchasing factor yet.
Interviewer:
How do relationships differ with large vs. small partners?
Interviewee:
Large firms are formally structured, but we try to make relationships personal. We visit each other, meet at trade fairs, exchange small gestures. Trust is very important. We prefer stable, long-term partners over constantly switching.
Interviewer:
And trust?
Interviewee:
It’s crucial. Without it, we don’t enter into long-term deals. With new suppliers, it takes time to build. Once trust is established, everything runs smoothly.
Interviewer:
How do you see symbiotic partnerships fitting in?
Interviewee:
For us, it would be natural to build them the same way we build other business relationships—on trust and personal connection. If we found the right partners, it would be a huge advantage: they’d be happy to get our surplus, we’d be happy to avoid disposal costs.
Interviewer:
And risks?
Interviewee:
The main risk is reliability. If we depend on one partner for taking or supplying surplus and they suddenly stop, we’re stuck. Diversification would be needed.
Interviewer:
So overall, you see strong potential?
Interviewee:
Yes. For us it would reduce costs and space problems, even if efficiency itself isn’t directly improved. Already that is significant.
Interviewer:
Thank you.
Interviewee:
Thank you.
