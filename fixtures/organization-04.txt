Organization: 4
Interviewer:
To begin, could you describe how your organization is structured? How is your internal organization designed?
Interviewee:
Yes, we are a non-profit institute. In our structure, we have the institute’s council, a director, and a professional council – a council of experts that advises both the institute’s council and the director. In addition, we have a “living laboratory,” which is a special unit within the structure. It includes members and associate members, free of charge – in a less formal, looser connection.
We also have a science section, divided into six departments, and supportive services. Altogether, we have 60 employees – interestingly, half of them are foreigners, coming from 20 different countries. The average age of employees is 38. The institute has existed for more than five and a half years.
Interviewer:
Very interesting. Would you say that your organization would be ready to participate in industrial symbiosis networks – even where stakeholders share investments and results?
Interviewee:
Yes, of course. We already collaborate on several levels – with industry, with the local community. We have direct cooperation, where we commercially offer our services. Another is through research and development projects, where industrial partners are also involved. The third level is events – workshops, lectures, and collaborations initiated by industry.
Our research focus is definitely oriented toward environmental benefits – we’re talking about renewable materials, the circular economy, and finding ways to make the built environment more environmentally friendly. For example – we cooperate locally with hotels: guests at reception calculate the carbon footprint of their arrival and then receive concrete suggestions on how to reduce it.
We have no problem cooperating with industry. Companies often come to us when they have a specific problem. What we notice, however, is a difference in mindset – abroad, companies strategically decide on development, without subsidies. In Slovenia, companies often ask: “Where can we get funding?” But yes, this is improving.
Interviewer:
How do you assess the current state and future goals regarding sustainability in Slovenia? Are you involved in any local sustainability partnerships?
Interviewee:
I think companies are already oriented toward sustainability – today, if you’re not, you’re not competitive. The question is how much of it is actually implemented. Most are aware of it, but it’s still more about opportunity-seeking than deep commitment to the environment.
And that’s why the role of politics is very important – legislation is improving, providing frameworks, and I think it is moving in the right direction.
Interviewer:
What are the advantages of your location? What kind of support do you receive from the community?
Interviewee:
To be honest – Slovenia alone cannot sustain us. We must also operate abroad. But the local environment strongly supported our development – we obtained building rights in Izola.
It’s true, however, that there aren’t many companies in the municipality itself. Nevertheless, cooperation is good. We have a new project – together with the municipality we published a call for ideas leading to startup development. We will help with mentoring and also with the use of our infrastructure.
Interviewer:
How do you assess the legislation on waste management and recycling?
Interviewee:
In general, it’s not that demanding, at least as far as our work is concerned. The real issue is what happens at collection points and how waste is then processed.
We work a lot on so-called reverse logistics – for example, how to return used wood into production chains. It requires decontamination, sorting, classification. The problem is that it’s easiest to classify everything as “other,” which leads to lower-quality raw materials.
We also have another challenge – since we work with laboratory chemicals, we generate hazardous waste. And the nearest authorized collectors are quite far away. But we connected with the local utility company and managed to solve it successfully.
Interviewer:
Which factors would you highlight as key for the successful implementation of sustainability projects?
Interviewee:
In our case, every project is defined already at the preparation stage in terms of SDGs – the Sustainable Development Goals. That way we know what the project contributes to.
After implementation, we focus on exploitation – dissemination of results, communication, practical application. That’s where it shows whether a project really delivers something.
Interviewer:
What do you think of Slovenia’s efforts to reduce emissions, water use, and waste?
Interviewee:
We have projects that deal with this. We work a lot with wood, with olive leaves – analyzing their chemical properties for pharmaceuticals, food. We also analyzed wastewater from weaving plants.
We also renovated a cultural monument – a monastery – with sustainable solutions.
Izola has twice received the Green Destination label, but we still face problems – especially with water resources. Drought, tourism, consumption… all of this has an impact. We try to address it with our calculators and by raising awareness among guests.
Interviewer:
How does knowledge exchange between you and companies take place? Is there enough education?
Interviewee:
Education is key – not only in companies, but also in the research sector and in education itself. The problem is that companies can’t afford for staff to be absent for additional training.
The circular economy is demanding – raw materials often appear only for a short time. Example: olive leaves – once a year.
Startups can hardly afford a production line that only runs one month per year. We lack physical infrastructure with pre-obtained permits.
That’s why I say – money is not the problem. Time is the problem. And legal infrastructure.
Interviewer:
Do these sustainability collaborations also include exchange of materials or resources for other processes?
Interviewee:
Partly, yes. But the biggest challenge is not the material itself, but the timing of availability and access to premises with permits.
That’s why circular startups have more difficulty implementing their ideas – larger companies with existing infrastructure have an advantage here.
Interviewer:
What about human capital? Do we have sufficient expertise in Slovenia?
Interviewee:
This is a very current topic. Young people today live to work, not the other way around. They want flexibility, less stress.
For the circular economy, an interdisciplinary team is needed – you can’t do everything yourself. Our team is like that – international, diverse. We have new ideas every day. And that is key.
Interviewer:
Who are your key partners?
Interviewee:
We cooperate with research institutions at home and abroad. Abroad, often within EU projects.
Also with industry – locally and nationally – we seek ways to cooperate. It’s important when a partner comes back to you. That means you did something right.
Interviewer:
How do you build trust with new partners?
Interviewee:
Often they find us themselves – through recommendations or online. Sometimes they don’t know exactly what they want to do, so we visit them and present ideas.
We put in a lot of effort – also with research visits. We invite researchers into the company, or the other way around. That’s how trust is built. It’s not always easy, especially with confidential industrial projects, but with openness and gradual progress, it works.
Interviewer:
What about formal and informal cooperation between companies – how does it work in Slovenia?
Interviewee:
Every institution has strategic partners – that’s fine. But cooperation must also take place at the operational level.
The problem is that everyone targets the same calls – ARIS projects are small, with many partners and little funding. Abroad we receive €500,000, at home €300,000 for four institutions over three years.
It is improving, larger calls are coming, but the path is still long.
Interviewer:
How would you assess the level of trust among stakeholders in industrial symbiosis?
Interviewee:
Trust exists. If you have a good idea, no one will just steal it. And if they do – then it wasn’t that good.
Of course, there are individuals with bad experiences, but in principle there is a lot of room for cooperation.
Interviewer:
Do you have any suggestions for improving industrial symbiosis networks?
Interviewee:
More informal meetings, more conversations, less bureaucracy. We need gradual approaches – not radical cuts.
It’s important that companies listen to each other and that they have space to start. Infrastructure with permits is essential.
Interviewer:
Do you believe you could further improve your efficiency through symbiotic cooperation?
Interviewee:
Of course. We try. But companies also need to trust – researchers, partners.
The research sector must be fast, responsive. We operate on the market – if we say something will be done in 14 days, it will be. Companies appreciate that. With universities it is sometimes more difficult – because they have many other obligations.
But abroad we see that research institutions cooperate much more with industry. We are also moving in this direction – with IPR development, with research projects that have concrete effects.
