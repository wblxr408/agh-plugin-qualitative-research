Organization: 15
Interviewer: To start, could you describe your organizational structure and how your activity is organized?
Interviewee: Of course. I am self-employed, and my primary work is in education and knowledge transfer – both at universities and in companies. I deliver lectures, workshops, consulting… all on sustainability, circular economy, and business strategies. I collaborate with universities and other experts to give students and employees a comprehensive perspective on these topics.
Interviewer: Would you be willing to take part in industrial symbiosis – a network where companies share resources, costs, and benefits?
Interviewee: Theoretically – absolutely yes. My activity produces very little waste or reusable resources, but I am convinced I could contribute through knowledge transfer and awareness-raising. I would say that such networks require a clear legal foundation and a very high level of trust among stakeholders. Infrastructure and logistics can be big obstacles – especially for small companies. But if there were willingness and support from various sides – academia and politics included – this could be pushed forward.
Interviewer: How do you assess Slovenia’s current sustainability goals? Are you part of any local initiatives?
Interviewee: The goals are well set – aligned with the Green Deal and Agenda 2030. Energy efficiency, waste reduction, care for natural resources… these are ongoing. Locally, however, it’s more focused on awareness and education – mainly in schools and at the household level. Unfortunately, I don’t yet see strong entrepreneurial networks in this area.
Interviewer: How supportive is the environment where you work? Would you say your location has advantages?
Interviewee: Definitely – access to universities, research institutes, and funding calls is an advantage. This enables participation in projects and content development. The state provides some incentives – but they would be far more effective if administrative barriers were smaller.
Interviewer: What’s your view of legislation – for example, waste and recycling laws?
Interviewee: I think Slovenia is aligned with EU directives – we have the Environmental Protection Act and so on. In my field I don’t experience this directly, but when collaborating on projects, the complexity of procedures and lengthy processes are very noticeable. Simplification would be very welcome.
Interviewer: Which factors do you see as key for successful sustainability projects?
Interviewee: Financing is crucial – affordable loans, subsidies. But also demand. If there are no buyers for sustainable products or services, businesses struggle to justify the transition. Legislation should also be more agile and faster. And – education is key. Without it, companies often don’t even know what options are available to them.
Interviewer: How is Slovenia progressing in reducing emissions, water use, and waste?
Interviewee: There is progress. Solar power plants, electric vehicles, waste separation – we see all of this. With water, however, I’d say it’s somewhat neglected. Since we have plenty of water, we take it for granted.
Interviewer: What about the relationship of the local environment toward companies pursuing sustainability goals?
Interviewee: There are initiatives, projects, and funding calls – but too much depends on individuals or enthusiastic groups. We would need more systematic networking and less paperwork. But I see great potential.
Interviewer: Are there examples of resource or cost exchange between companies?
Interviewee: I know of some examples – e.g. sawdust from sawmills going to paper mills, waste textiles into insulation materials. But often new materials are still cheaper than recycled ones. Legislation is also not always adapted to such exchanges.
Interviewer: What human capital is needed for successful industrial symbiosis?
Interviewee: We need people with technical, economic, and legal knowledge – but also communication skills. Slovenia has an educated workforce, but systemic support is missing. Links between industry and academia should be stronger and more focused.
Interviewer: Who do you usually collaborate with? How did these collaborations develop?
Interviewee: Mostly with universities and the renewable energy sector. I usually establish connections myself. For projects, knowledge and common goals are key – often also for securing funding.
Interviewer: How important is trust in these relationships?
Interviewee: Extremely important. Work with universities and companies often involves confidential information – security is essential. Without transparency and respect for agreements, cooperation doesn’t work.
Interviewer: How do you see formal vs. informal collaboration among companies?
Interviewee: I see growth in collaboration. Formal – contracts. Informal – networking at events. But if subsidies were more accessible and procedures simpler, even more companies would collaborate.
Interviewer: How does trust work in the context of industrial symbiosis?
Interviewee: There is a lot of hesitation – mainly about sharing business data. Companies fear exposing key information. Where relationships are already established – e.g. in the renewable energy sector – it’s easier. But I still miss a clear legal framework and reduced economic risks.
Interviewer: Do you have concrete suggestions for improving these processes?
Interviewee: Digital platforms for information exchange, clear legislation, infrastructure in industrial zones for shared resource use, more workshops with good practices. And investment in knowledge – in companies, schools, institutions.
Interviewer: And finally – do you think you can still improve your own efficiency?
Interviewee: Absolutely. By reducing paper use, increasing digital tools, and joining local initiatives. But my biggest contribution is connecting education with business – and raising awareness of the importance of sustainable practices.
