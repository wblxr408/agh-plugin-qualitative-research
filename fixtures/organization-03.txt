Organization: 3
Interviewer:
What is your organizational structure, can you describe it?
Interviewee:
Our organizational structure is very flat. Of course, every company formally has a director, but in our case, jobs are divided into three groups: consultants, project managers, and professional associates. We don’t have formal departments, because we don’t want people to isolate themselves in their own segment. We want more open collaboration. Even spatially, our offices are organized so that eight people share one office – this makes the flow of information easier. We are a small organization with about 35 employees.
Interviewer:
Would you be willing to participate in an industrial symbiosis network, where financial investments and results are shared?
Interviewee:
Absolutely. We already operate in a way that creates synergies. The Rudolfovo institute itself was created as a result of staff sharing – part of the team restructured into a new organization, which made the start easier.
We are not an industrial company – we don’t have large stocks or materials, our main resource is employees. And in this segment, we are very active – we collaborate with more than five employers. For example: with the school system, with Institute Y we share an employee, with Organization XX we have two more shared jobs, we also collaborate with Organization XY and others.
Sharing employees is normal for us – it brings knowledge, competences, and broader collaboration. It’s not easy, it requires commitment, but the effects are very positive.
Interviewer:
What are the current and future sustainability goals in your environment? Are you involved in any local or cross-sectoral sustainability collaborations?
Interviewee:
Yes, such collaborations already exist. I remember one founding session of the Section for Environment and Space, where I emphasized the importance of personal contact. In Slovenia, it’s harder to establish cooperation with someone you don’t know. For example, one representative from Company X said they were buying waste glass from Ljubljana, and another from Krka said they were transporting glass there – and they connected. The result? Cost savings, reduced transport, better utilization.
We try to bring together different stakeholders into projects where synergies can emerge. That is one of our main approaches.
Interviewer:
What are the advantages of your location in Slovenia? What kind of support do you receive from the community?
Interviewee:
We are in a position where we have good access to information – also from the EU. For example, the EU has long been discussing zero-construction – meaning no more building on greenfield land. Only on degraded areas or where something already stands. We know this and include such information in development strategies for municipalities.
We also often detect local opportunities. For example, the Novo mesto airport – it was unused for a long time, but we recognized a possibility for development. We cooperate with municipalities, clubs, and investors. We include environmental studies (for example on the pond turtle) and develop plans.
Also with other projects – for example robotics clubs, ideas for a velodrome, sports infrastructure. We are always looking at what the environment needs and how we can support it.
Interviewer:
How do you assess the legislative framework in the field of recycling and secondary use?
Interviewee:
Legislation is often written by people in offices, with no contact with reality. It becomes impractical. It often punishes those who want to do things properly, while others bypass it.
Example: in the subsidy for solar power plants, the contract was so complex that it was almost impossible to follow all the provisions. Too much bureaucracy. What we need are simpler procedures, where someone takes responsibility and says: “This complies, this does not.”
Right now, the system is too rigid, pragmatism is lacking.
Interviewer:
Which factors do you see as key for the successful implementation of sustainability projects?
Interviewee:
First: the environment itself must have the right conditions. Then: someone must have the idea. And then: all stakeholders must be informed and trust each other.
I would add time. And awareness. Our grandparents didn’t have the word sustainability, but they still acted sustainably.
If we realize that the world does not exist just for us, that we are only one link in the chain, sustainability will gain another meaning.
Interviewer:
How does your location contribute to reducing emissions, water use, and waste?
Interviewee:
We are often the first ones to generate an idea – for example, now we are developing a project with a sawmill and the municipality. Forest as a natural resource, the sawmill, cogeneration – the municipality would contribute biomass from public areas, we would set up a community power plant.
It would supply schools, the cultural center, and also companies. Less transport, less energy loss. The project is still in the early phase, but we have already connected the key stakeholders. Now we are building further.
Interviewer:
How do you assess Slovenia’s current attitude towards companies that include sustainability measures? Are there exchanges of knowledge?
Interviewee:
Too often, things are assessed only on the basis of price. If a colleague does an analysis and the only criterion is cost, I’m disappointed.
We also organize cultural events – European films, collaboration with LIFFe. We included Anton Podbevšek Theatre. These are non-commercial contents, but they enrich the environment.
We also contributed funds – so they could upgrade their equipment. These are broader societal benefits, not just economic.
Interviewer:
Do these relations also include exchange of products or resources for other processes?
Interviewee:
Of course. UN and EU guidelines are good, but the economy moves slower.
The problem is that we look too narrowly – only through the lens of economics. Regional agencies and chambers have an important role. Our region cooperates better than the average.
We also had the idea of heating a swimming pool with waste heat from Revoz. It didn’t succeed. Now it is heated with heat pumps.
The idea of a swimming pool at the School Center – I also helped to draft that. But for realization, persistence is needed.
Interviewer:
What human capital is needed for successful symbiosis between companies? Does it exist in your environment?
Interviewee:
It’s not about raising people, but about incentives for companies.
Good companies have drawers full of projects – they just need the opportunity to pull them out.
If you come with funds and say – “Here’s a call, are you interested?” – a good company will react.
Incentives are what shift priorities. The state, chambers, regions – that’s their area of influence.
Interviewer:
Who are your key partners? How has cooperation with them worked?
Interviewee:
It varies – by size, organizational form, location. We develop ideas, and we need resources for experts.
For example, a child comes to a summer robotics workshop, gets inspired. In 10 or 15 years, they will be an engineer. That long-term effect is not directly measurable, but it is real.
Example: the railway project we are now developing – by the time it really comes to life, I will probably already be retired.
Interviewer:
How important is trust in cooperation? How do you build it?
Interviewee:
Trust requires: (1) that you actually do something, not just talk; (2) that you are transparent and fair in attributing credit; (3) that everyone contributes something.
It doesn’t work if one pulls and the others only benefit. We also try to pass this on to younger generations – because solidarity still exists, but it is no longer self-evident.
Interviewer:
What is the general opinion on formal and informal cooperation?
Interviewee:
Formalization is important – for the file. In practice, if we know a company and trust them, things can also run without a contract. But contracts come later.
It’s always about the content first. Only then does formality follow.
Interviewer:
What is your assessment of trust among stakeholders in industrial symbiosis?
Interviewee:
As I already mentioned – trust must have foundations: work, transparency, and cooperation. There are no shortcuts.
Interviewer:
Do you have any suggestions for improving existing or new industrial symbiosis networks?
Interviewee:
It has to start with communication. Informal meetings, awareness-raising. Time is needed.
Even packaging producers must have the possibility to adapt. I support gradual changes. Not fast and radical ones, except when absolutely necessary.
Companies must be open – not only to receive, but also to share information.
Interviewer:
Do you think you can still improve efficiency within your organization?
Interviewee:
Yes. Our focus is: technology, sustainability, and mobility. Also, the aging population.
Our clients are local communities and companies.
Even before the electricity crisis, we replaced all the lights. We raise awareness among employees to switch off lights.
We adjusted the systems, and the results are visible in the bills.
We reduce paper consumption, introduced temperature standards, and invest in improvements.
But we still face challenges – packaging, vending machines with cups. We are working on solutions.
